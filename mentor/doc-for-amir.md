# COMP3000 Proposal (VidBriefs (2.0))

## 1. Overview
VidBriefs (2.0); an innovative idea created to be unprecedented in how users interact with and consume video content. Essentially, it is made for people who wish to consume the video's insights efficiently without having to watch the entire video. I will leverage advanced Deep Learning techniques, in a high-level way whilst utilising APIs, to ensure that the logged-in user receives personalised responses tailored to their viewing habits and preferences.

## 1.1 Idea and Why
Early post injury, I found it hard to concentrate, particularly on things that I don't necessarily care about (engage into), e.g., *why am I even intrigued to watch this in the first place?* What did I want to learn or gain from the video? I therefore created *VidBriefs* wherein I utilised the `youtube-transcript-api` on Python to extract video transcripts from their respective YouTube video link, then fed it into `gpt-3.5-turbo` or `gpt-4` in a looped conversation wherein the AI will answer specific questions and provide concise summaries based on my interests and queries.

The other half of the motivation is that YouTube rewards long videos (the longer people watch, the more the creator gets paid), so a 40-minute video oftentimes contains about 5 minutes of what I actually came for. Essentially the problem is not "summarise this video", it is "get me what I came for, faster", and ideally before I have even had to work out how to ask for it.

## 1.2. What Is Different
### 1.2.1 VidBriefs 1.0
This was made as an iOS app which initially would just extract the youtube video transcript from the link, feed it to AI, then allow the user to prompt the AI with specific questions regarding it. 

However, this was created primarily for my personal use where I did not delve deeper as the main idea was finalised in my eyes: if I want to learn something from a YouTube video without watching the whole thing, AI can just read the transcript in seconds and provide me with the key insights concerning what I specifically asked about (not just a general summary).
### 1.2.2 VidBriefs 2.0
VidBriefs (2.0) takes this innovative concept further by incorporating more advanced AI techniques: 1) learn user preferences and viewing habits over time to provide increasingly personalised summaries of not just certain videos/channels, but also *chapters* of the video within the video (this could perhaps utilise youtube's own chapters along with the transcript-content of each video combined), 2) utilise more sophisticated natural language processing models to generate higher-quality insights, 3) answer a user's question by pointing to the exact timestamped parts of the video that answer it, 4) learn from how the user treats each extracted part (played through, skipped, replayed) instead of only asking them to rate anything (with a slider which could be hidden into another interface element so the user doesn't feel like they are manually rating), 5) use the video itself and not only the transcript, so things shown but not said are captured, 6) generate a few questions at the end, both to check what they learnt and to learn what they care about 7) create the video-chat with an overview of the questions etc

### 1.2.3 What already exists, and what is actually different
I am not claiming nobody has done video summarisation; it is a mature area and I am building on it rather than recreating it. From what I have looked at so far: NotebookLM imports a YouTube transcript and answers questions on it, YouTube already has its own "Ask" button (Gemini) on the watch page, free and live in the UK, and there are many browser extensions (e.g. Eightify, NoteGPT) that give a generic summary with timestamps. In the literature, generic video summarisation (the TVSum and SumMe datasets) picks the "important" parts for everyone, query-focused summarisation (Sharghi et al., 2017) picks the parts that answer a question you type in, and the closest thing to my idea is HiPPO-Video (2025), which learns personalised highlights from a person's watch history.

The difference, as I see it, is that all of the above wait to be asked: the user has to type the question (or watch the video) every single time. None of the ones I have found try to predict what *this* user will want from a video *before* they ask, from how they treated previous videos in the app, and then lead the summary with that. So underneath I am using the same kind of tools as everyone else (transcript extraction, an existing LLM for the writing) and adding one component on top: the part that learns the user. If it turns out somebody has done exactly this, then that becomes a build-on and a comparison, not a replication.

Regarding the current challenges in this area, as I understand them: 1) "important" is subjective and there is no ground truth, which is why the public datasets use averaged human scores, 2) a transcript loses whatever is shown on screen but not said, 3) personalisation needs a history per user, and no public dataset has that for this exact task (HiPPO-Video's histories are mostly simulated), 4) what the user does is a biased signal, e.g. they cannot skip a part they never saw in the first place.

## 1.3. Methodology

1. Extract video transcript using `youtube-transcript-api`, as well as visual content via `gemini-3.8-flash`.
   - then split the video into parts with timestamps, using YouTube's own chapters where they exist, otherwise chunks of the transcript
2. Feed the video's content:
   - Jev: the first system-one AI that won't generate text, but will be utilised as an invisible engine which supports the master AI's understanding, thus response?
      - **Input**: the parts of the video plus what this person asked before and if they played, skipped or replayed anything
      - **Output**: a relevance-score for each part of the video scored on its importance aligned with the user's interests and previous interactions, and which previous question or interaction it matched, so the master AI can say why it led with that part
   - Reading-AI or reading-panel: the AI(s) that directly get and consider the video(s) content (utilise `gemini-3.8-flash` if video is to be visually analysed too).
   - Master AI: an existing model used via its API which reads the parts Jev scored highest, then what the reading-AI or reading-panel deemed most relevant, and generates the final summary that answers the user.

   perhaps the user could also provide feedback on the final summary, which would then be used to further refine the AI's understanding and improve future summaries?
3. Rank the parts by Jev's score and keep only the top few (a fixed reading budget, e.g. 5 parts), which is all the master AI gets, along with the user's question if they asked one.
4. The master AI writes the answer/summary with each point linked to its timestamp, so the user can jump straight to that part of the video. If the visual side (point 5) is in, Gemini only looks at the kept parts' timestamps, not the whole video, so the cost stays small.
5. Log what the user then does with each part (opened, skipped, replayed, re-read, asked a follow-up) and feed that back into Jev as training data for the next video.

Note regarding Jev, since it is the only part I would write myself: it is not a new kind of model or architecture. It takes the vectors from an existing pretrained sentence encoder (off the shelf, e.g. `sentence-transformers`) for each part of the video and for the user's previous questions, and a small network on top learns a score from them. On the side, as a learning exercise, I would like to write out the maths of that small network myself (forward pass, loss, derivatives) and check it against PyTorch, because I want to actually understand it before postgrad; the version that ships is standard PyTorch either way, and the target of the project is the summarisation, i.e. does the user get what they came for faster, and Jev is judged on that, not as a machine learning contribution compared against other architectures.

## 1.4 How I decide which parts of a video are important
### 1.4.1 Without personalisation (one version for everyone)
1) Split: YouTube chapters where the creator made them, otherwise fixed chunks of the transcript (roughly a minute each), keeping the real timestamps. 2) A generic importance score per part from existing methods: how connected the part is to the rest of the transcript (existing methods such as TextRank do this on the sentence vectors), plus an LLM rating each part out of 10 for "does this carry a fact, step or conclusion", plus YouTube's own "most replayed" graph where it shows one (a free crowd signal of what everyone else jumped to; yt-dlp returns it as 100 timestamped values per video, checked on three videos), minus intros, sponsor segments and outros. 3) This generic score on its own is what a brand new user gets, and it is also the baseline that everything personal is compared against.

### 1.4.2 With personalisation
Jev adds a personal score on top of the generic one: how similar each part is to what this user asked before (vector similarity to their past questions, weighted towards recent ones), and how they treated similar parts before (replayed or re-read pushes it up, skipped pushes it down, albeit skipped is treated as weak evidence since they may never have seen it). Final score = generic + personal, with the weighting between the two learned from the data rather than set by me. For a brand new user there is no history, so they get the generic score plus the few questions asked at sign-up (point 6 in 1.2.2), and the personal part switches on as their history builds.

## 1.5 Where the data comes from
1) Videos and transcripts: any YouTube video via `youtube-transcript-api` (with timestamps), plus the roughly 6,000 TED talk transcripts I already collected for VidBriefs 2. 2) The interaction history, which is the data that actually matters for Jev: nobody has a public dataset of this for my app, so it gets logged from day one, firstly me, via a small logging endpoint on the Django backend and a plain web page, so it does not wait for the Android app to be finished. Pilot users (friends and family) come second and need the module's ethics form, which I would submit at the start of semester 1 so it is not on the critical path. 3) Public datasets to check Jev against, and to bootstrap the "which part answers this question" half: QVHighlights (2021, questions paired with the moments that answer them; about 8 GB of precomputed features, 144 GB with the videos), HiPPO-Video (2025, 2,040 watch histories, but they are LLM-simulated rather than real people, about 270 MB), PHD-GIFs (2018, real users' previous highlight picks with user IDs, about 9 MB of annotations, the videos fetched separately from YouTube), and TVSum/SumMe for the generic importance (about 3.2 GB together). All of these are downloadable today without a request form (checked 9 October); none of them has real per-user histories for my exact task except PHD-GIFs, and that is a different signal (GIF picks, not questions).

## 1.6 How I will know it worked
The claim is "Jev predicts what you would want from a video", so the test is: hide what the user actually asked or did on a later video, and see whether Jev ranked those parts at the top. This is measured with nDCG@5 (essentially, how high up the list the parts the user actually found useful came, looking at the top five). Baselines it has to beat: 1) generic score only, no personalisation, 2) just the first few minutes of the video, 3) keyword match to past questions, 4) vector similarity to past questions with no learning at all, 5) an existing LLM given a short text profile of the user. Plus one sanity check: give Jev somebody else's history; if it does just as well, the personalisation is not doing anything. The main evidence is my own logged history plus the public datasets; the pilot users are a second layer on top if the ethics approval comes through in time. All of this gets fixed before any training happens, and I would report where it fails as well as where it works.
